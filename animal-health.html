A program of The Jopetnah Foundation

Healthy animals. Safe families.

Rabies is preventable, yet it still kills people in Uganda, almost always after a dog bite. The Jopetnah Animal Health Initiative is our plan to protect families by vaccinating community dogs and cats, training local animal-health workers and linking bite victims to treatment.

Our approach: We treat the health of household animals as a public-health, livelihood and household-stability issue. Vaccination and community trust come first. We do not cull dogs, because culling destroys the trust that prevention depends on.
Current Status (September 2026): In design. We are developing partnerships and preparing a pilot in northern Uganda. We are not yet vaccinating animals, and we are not accepting donations for this initiative. The Foundation's legal registration in Uganda is in progress.

Why it matters

A preventable disease that still takes lives.

Once symptoms appear, rabies is almost always fatal. It can be prevented, and the most effective way to stop it is to vaccinate dogs, which cause more than 99% of human cases worldwide, and to make sure anyone who is bitten reaches treatment quickly.

Uganda has a national plan to eliminate dog-mediated rabies, in line with the global Zero by 30 goal. Delivering it in remote districts depends on local partners who can reach households, keep vaccines cold and report bites. That is the gap this initiative is designed to help close.

What the evidence says

  • 190recorded human rabies deaths in Uganda between 2021 and 2024. Surveillance is weak, so few cases are recorded.
  • About 90%of Ugandans live in areas where dogs can transmit rabies, while dog vaccination and access to treatment remain low.
  • 2030the global goal to end human deaths from dog-mediated rabies, set by WHO, FAO, WOAH and GARC (Zero by 30).
  • 2025–2030Uganda's National Rabies Elimination Strategy, published jointly by the Ministry of Agriculture, Animal Industry and Fisheries and the Ministry of Health.

Sources: Okech et al., Frontiers in Tropical Diseases (2025); Uganda rabies information project; Ministry of Agriculture, Animal Industry and Fisheries; WHO. Facts last checked 20 September 2026.

Our plan

Vaccinate, train, connect and learn.

Four proposed program strands, designed to work through existing health and community structures rather than alongside them.

Design Stage

Rabies Zero

A proposed vaccination and bite-response program for dogs and cats, run from or alongside health centres. A 2025 Ugandan pilot found this approach reached the highest coverage.

Focus

  • Dog and cat vaccination campaigns
  • Bite response: awareness, referral, treatment support and reporting
  • Data shared with district veterinary and health officers
Design Stage

Community Animal Health Workers

Local workers trained, equipped and supervised to deliver routine vaccination and basic care, earning a fair income so services continue after any grant ends.

Focus

  • Community nomination and training
  • Starter kits and cold-chain equipment
  • Referral to licensed veterinarians
  • Rabies vaccination free; basic treatment low-cost
Design Stage

Schools and Faith Communities

Bite prevention, first aid and basic animal care taught through schools and faith communities. Children are among those most affected, so schools are a natural place to start.

Focus

  • Bite-prevention and first-aid sessions
  • School-based vaccination days
  • Community and faith-leader awareness events
Design Stage

Evidence and Partnerships

A baseline study, simple monitoring and a university partnership to measure what changes and to share results with district and national authorities.

Focus

  • Baseline study of dog ownership, bites and costs
  • Clinical leadership through a veterinary partner
  • Learning shared openly, including what does not work
Indicative pilot goals (subject to funding & partnerships): Reach at least 65% dog vaccination coverage in each pilot district (aiming for 70%), train and equip 30–45 Community Animal Health Workers, and support at least 80% of reported bite cases to complete post-exposure treatment. Targets will be confirmed after a baseline study. We aim to contribute to Uganda's national elimination target, not to claim it alone.
How it fits

A One Health thread, not a fifth pillar.

The health of people, animals and the environment is connected. The initiative runs through The Jopetnah Foundation's four pillars, so the same families can benefit from support across their whole lives, in line with our integrated model.

Health & Wellbeing

Vaccination, bite response and referral sit alongside Community Health Champions and Mobile Wellness Clinics.

Education & Youth Development

School sessions teach bite prevention and first aid.

Livelihoods

Community Animal Health Workers build local skills, income and small enterprises.

Environment & Community Development

Stray-animal and waste management links to community clean-up work.

Safety first

If you or someone you know is bitten.

  1. Wash the wound straight away with soap and running water for at least 15 minutes.
  2. Go to the nearest health facility right away, even if the wound looks small, so a health worker can decide on post-exposure treatment.
  3. Do not wait for symptoms. Rabies treatment works only before symptoms begin.
  4. Tell the local veterinary or health officer about the animal.

This is general guidance and not a substitute for medical advice. Follow the instructions of your local health workers.
